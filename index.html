A field guide to quantitative finance

What does a quant actually do?

Quants use maths, statistics and code to make trading decisions. Nobody can say which way a price goes next. A quant measures how wide the range of outcomes is, then hunts for small edges that repeat across thousands of trades.

Four kinds of quant

Titles change from firm to firm, but most quant teams split the work along these lines. The colour of each role is used throughout the page to show who owns what.

Quant researcher

“Is there a pattern in this data that will still be there next month?”

Spends the day
Cleaning data, forming hypotheses, running backtests, reading papers, and throwing away most ideas.
Tools
Python (pandas, NumPy), statistics, SQL, notebooks
Usual background
Maths, physics, statistics or computer science, often a PhD

Quant trader

“How much should we bet, and when do we stop?”

Spends the day
Running live strategies, sizing positions, watching fills and risk, switching off anything that misbehaves.
Tools
Live dashboards, Python, probability, quick mental maths
Usual background
Maths, engineering or economics. Interviews test betting games and fast estimates.

Quant developer

“Will this run correctly, every time, in a few microseconds?”

Spends the day
Building data pipelines, backtest engines, exchange connections and order systems, then measuring and removing delay.
Tools
C++, Python, Linux, networking. FPGAs at the fastest firms.
Usual background
Computer science or software engineering

Risk & pricing quant

“What is this contract worth, and how much could we lose?”

Spends the day
Building models that price derivatives, working out hedges and loss limits, checking other people’s models.
Tools
Probability, stochastic calculus, Monte Carlo, C++ or Python
Usual background
Maths or physics. Common at banks and insurers.

    Signals · backtesting · overfitting

    Test a trading rule, then see if it was luck

    ResearcherDeveloper

    The rule: hold the stock when its short moving average is above its long one, otherwise sit in cash. The first two years are in-sample, the data you are allowed to look at while choosing settings. The last year is out-of-sample, held back as an honest test.

    “Nothing” is a pure random walk: no rule can truly beat it. “Real trends” adds up and down drifts that last about three months.

    The same backtest in 10 lines of Python 
    import pandas as pd

def backtest(prices: pd.Series, fast=10, slow=60, cost_bps=5):
    position = (prices.rolling(fast).mean() > prices.rolling(slow).mean()).astype(int)
    trades = position.diff().abs().fillna(0)
    daily = prices.pct_change()
    # act on tomorrow's return: using today's would be looking into the future
    strat = position.shift(1) * daily - trades.shift(1) * cost_bps / 10_000
    sharpe = strat.mean() / strat.std() * 252 ** 0.5
    return strat, sharpe

    Derivatives · Black–Scholes · Greeks

    Putting a price on uncertainty

    Pricing quantTrader

    An option is a contract that lets you buy (a call) or sell (a put) a stock at a fixed price, the strike, on a future date. If the deal is bad on that date you simply walk away. The Black–Scholes formula, published in 1973, says what that choice is worth today.

    What to notice

    • The grey hockey stick is what the option pays on the final day. The blue curve is its fair value today.
    • Raise volatility: the curve lifts away from the stick. More uncertainty makes the option worth more.
    • Drag days to 1: the curve collapses onto the stick as the time value runs out.

    What quant developers build
    • Market data handlersRead millions of exchange messages a second and rebuild the order book.
    • Order gatewaysSend, amend and cancel orders, and check every one against risk limits first.
    • Backtest enginesReplay years of history quickly and without peeking at the future.
